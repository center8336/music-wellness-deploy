Performing and Creative Artists

Frontiers in Psychology, 16, 1329029

DOI: 10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1329029 · PMID 40918295

Reviewed by 陳棉 · 2026-10-12

↓ Scroll
Part A — Framing

Section · 01

Why Read This

Read the Frontiers Paper →

The queue reached for this paper as the synthesis item and as a structural parallel to this project’s own Taiwan-gap argument. It is both — and the parallel is sharper than expected, though not in the direction intended.

The review’s argument shape is the one we use: the construct is undefined, unmeasured, and has no validated instrument in this population, therefore no intervention here can be evaluated. That shape is sound and transplants cleanly.

The review’s execution of it is the cautionary half. A paper whose central complaint is that nobody defines the construct consistently builds its own analysis on a four-part framework that neither of the two sources it cites for that framework actually lists.

Read it for the gap claim and for the instrument warning. Do not read its counts as a map of the literature.

Section · 02

What This Paper Is

A scoping review, following PRISMA-ScR, of how mental health literacy is conceptualised, operationalised and measured in the performing- and creative-arts health literature.1

Databases were searched from inception to the first week of June 2025. 669 records were screened, 53 sought at full text, 50 assessed, and 26 studies included — published 1997–2024, spanning nine countries, with at least 4,710 participants, of whom 1,502 (32%) were musicians and 3,848 (82%) were students.12

Findings are organised against four components of mental health literacy: recognition of disorders; knowledge of risk factors and of information and support services; attitudes promoting help-seeking; and understanding of stigma.

The headline results: one included study defined mental health literacy, one covered all four components, stigma was the least examined component and almost exclusively in musicians, and no validated instrument for mental health literacy in artists exists. The authors additionally consulted eight knowledge stakeholders in Canada and the US — a mix of leaders in performing-arts-medicine research and practice, working performing and creative artists, and clinicians who specialise in caring for artists — none of whom could identify any literature on the topic.1

Received 2023-10-27, accepted 2025-06-25, published 2025-08-22, in the Performance Science section under the ISPS 2023 Research Topic. No correction or erratum is attached as of this reading.10

The null stakeholder consultation is, quietly, the most robust finding in the paper — eight people positioned to know, asked directly, knew of nothing. It is reported in Methods rather than Results, so it is not counted among the findings at all.

Part B — What Does Not Hold

Section · 03

The Framework Is the Authors’ Own

This is the central defect. The review’s four components are introduced as established: “(i) recognition of mental health disorders, (ii) knowledge of their risk factors and of seeking mental health information and support services, (iii) attitudes that promote recognition and appropriate help-seeking; and (iv) understanding the concept of stigma,” cited to Jorm (2000) and Sampaio et al. (2022).1 Figure 1’s caption reads “Four components of mental health literacy (Jorm, 2000)”; the Methods state “we used the existing components of mental health literacy (Jorm, 2000) as a guiding framework.”1

Jorm (2000) enumerates six components, not four. Verbatim: “(a) the ability to recognise specific disorders or different types of psychological distress; (b) knowledge and beliefs about risk factors and causes; (c) knowledge and beliefs about self-help interventions; (d) knowledge and beliefs about professional help available; (e) attitudes which facilitate recognition and appropriate help-seeking; and (f) knowledge of how to seek mental health information.”4 Stigma is not among them.

Sampaio et al. (2022) reproduces Jorm’s six almost verbatim and cites them to Jorm. “Stigma” appears once in that paper — as an outcome that knowledge-based interventions fail to shift, not as a component.5

So the fourth component — the one the review finds least examined — is listed in neither cited source. Items (i)–(iii) are a defensible collapse of Jorm’s six; item (iv) is an addition the paper makes without a source for it, attributed to a 25-year-old paper that does not list it.

The fair qualification. Jorm’s text does discuss stigma — the section headed “ATTITUDES THAT FACILITATE RECOGNITION AND HELP-SEEKING” opens “There is a stigma associated with mental disorders and this may hinder seeking help”4 — so item (iv) is readable as an unlisted split-out of Jorm’s component (e) rather than as a concept foreign to him. Li et al. also cite Kutcher, Wei & Coniglio (2016) elsewhere in the Discussion, the plausible origin of the four-part regrouping and of the “help-seeking efficacy” wording, though that paper is not open access and could not be checked here.1

The defect is not that stigma is alien to the construct. It is that a paper whose thesis is unstandardised conceptualisation presents a four-item list as established and cites it to two sources that each enumerate six.

Whoever presents this should say so at the top, because the irony is load-bearing: the review’s thesis is that the field suffers from unstandardised conceptualisation, and its own four-part conceptualisation is not sourced to any list that contains all four items.

Section · 04

The Counts Measure the Coding

The review’s quantitative spine — 13 studies covering one component, 8 two, 4 three, 1 all four — reads as a coverage map.1 It is not. Three things establish this.

1. The framework was applied retrospectively to studies that never used it. Few of the 26 studies set out to measure the review’s four components, and the paper itself reports that among the 18 studies with a quantitative component, none measured all four. The review’s own prose concedes this at least nine times — “this was not explicit”; “although not stated in the article, this could imply”; “we decided to include this component as representative of operationalizing mental health literacy”; “it was unclear whether the intervention had any focus on mental health risks”; “it is unclear whether these interventions are specifically targeting mental health promotion.”1 A count assembled this way cannot distinguish the literature covers this component from we could read this component into it.

2. The exemplars make the point concretely. The single study coded as covering all four components is Perkins et al. (2017) — twenty semi-structured interviews with current and recent students at six UK conservatoires, examining perceived enablers and barriers to health.7 The review’s own supplementary table records of it: “Mental health literacy is not defined in this study.”3

The single study that does define the construct is Barnett et al. (2019) — a touring rural art exhibition in Australia whose purpose was community stigma reduction. Of its 168 participants, 23 were artists and 145 were exhibition visitors, and its definition is a seven-word phrase, “knowledge of mental health issues and services,” covering at most part of one of the four components.36

The finding “only one study defines mental health literacy” is therefore weaker than it reads: the one definition is not a usable definition, and the one four-component study disclaims the construct.

3. The tallies do not reconcile with each other. Summed across components, the conceptualisation sections report 9 + 14 + 10 + 7 = 40 component-instances; the 13/8/4/1 distribution implies 45. The operationalisation sections report 7 + 7 + 6 + 4 = 24, against a stated “All studies in our review operationalized at least one component” and a distribution in which 13 studies touch two or more — arithmetically impossible. The stigma paragraph says seven studies, “four of which were in music, one in visual arts, and one in dance” — six.1

Section · 05

Numbers That Do Not Reconcile

Published, uncorrected, in the version of record:

The abstract and the results disagree on the headline distribution. Abstract: “Sixteen articles included one of the four components of mental health literacy, nine included two, four included three, and one had all four components” — 16 + 9 + 4 + 1 = 30, against 26 included studies. Results: “Thirteen articles (46.2.%) … eight (30.7%) … four (15.4%) … and one (5%)” — 13 + 8 + 4 + 1 = 26.1 The Results counts are the arithmetically consistent set — though, as below, their percentages are not. The abstract’s counts are consistent with nothing, and the abstract is what will be cited.

The Results percentages contradict the Results counts. 13/26 is 50.0%, not 46.2% (46.2% is 12/26); 8/26 is 30.8%, not 30.7%; 1/26 is 3.8%, not 5%.1 The bracket after “Thirteen” lists fourteen citations, because Bartos 2022a and 2022b are both named; the supplementary table counts Bartos once.13

The PRISMA diagram does not balance. Appendix 3 gives 50 reports assessed for eligibility, 23 ineligible, 26 included — 50 − 23 = 27. Its own exclusion sub-reasons (4 + 12 + 2 + 2 + 4) sum to 24, not 23.2 Since 50 − 24 = 26, and 26 is the included count, the likelier error is the “23” box total rather than the included total.

The Methods sentence invites the wrong reading of the paper’s own flow diagram. The Methods say 44 handsearched papers were found “eligible for full-text screening (n = 53),” which is readable as 44 of the 53 coming from handsearching.1 The diagram gives the actual split: of the 53 sought at full text, 41 came from the databases and 12 from handsearching.2 (44 is the number identified by handsearching; the rest were excluded at title and abstract.) The sentence is ambiguous rather than false — “n = 53” can be read as the total pool — but it is ambiguous in the one place a reader would check whether the designed search actually worked. The Methods’ own “625 unique citations” does reconcile with the diagram (691 − 62 − 4 = 625); it is the abstract’s “669 unique citations” that sits oddly against it, 669 being the screened total once the 44 handsearch records are added.

The arithmetic the review does report is correct. Of its participant figures, 1,502/4,710 = 31.9%, 608/4,710 = 12.9%, 276/4,710 = 5.9%, 3,848/4,710 = 81.7%, and 6/26 = 23% all check out.1 The defect is in the counts of studies, not of people — though “most studies included students as the participants (n = 3,848; 82%)” presents a participant count as a study count.1

Section · 06

Three Lists of the Databases Searched

The abstract names seven: MEDLINE, CINAHL, PubMed, EMBASE, PsycINFO, Web of Science, Cochrane. The Methods name six: MEDLINE, EMBASE, PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science, PsycINFO — dropping Cochrane.1 The PRISMA diagram identifies records from five: Cochrane 108, CINAHL 93, EMBASE 101, MEDLINE 220, PsycINFO 169 — restoring Cochrane and dropping both Web of Science and PubMed.2

The search-strategy section says the MEDLINE strategy “was adapted to PubMed, PsycINFO, and EMBASE databases,” and is silent on CINAHL, Web of Science and Cochrane.1

A reader cannot determine which databases were searched. For a scoping review whose entire claim is the extent of a literature, and whose search strategy was built with a university librarian and reported as following the PRESS guidelines, this is the most consequential reproducibility failure in the paper: the search cannot be repeated.

Relatedly, the nine countries are never named.1 The supplementary table does carry a country column — but it is labelled “Setting, Country of Publication,” and that is what it records. Bartos is set in Granada, Spain and published in the USA; Harrison’s setting reads “location not specified – Australia?” against a publication country of the Netherlands; Kaufman is the School of American Ballet, published in the United Kingdom.3 A column of publication countries cannot establish that the included studies were based in nine countries. The Results make a setting claim; the only country data supplied is a publication-venue claim, and the nine-country figure therefore cannot be reconstructed from the material in the paper.

Section · 07

Eligibility Criteria at War With Themselves

Inclusion: “Articles could be of any study design or publication (peer-reviewed articles, conference abstracts, protocols, dissertations and theses, and book chapters).”

Exclusion, criterion (v): publications that “are not peer-reviewed.”

Limitations: “our review can be at risk of publication bias, as we relied on peer-reviewed literature, potentially excluding unpublished or non-traditional forms of scholarship.”1

All three cannot hold. The included set settles it in favour of the inclusion arm: Washington-Simon (2024) is a doctoral dissertation.9 The limitations paragraph therefore claims a restriction the review did not apply, and apologises for a bias it partly avoided.

Two further reporting mismatches. PRISMA-ScR is named as the reporting guideline, but the flow diagram supplied is labelled PRISMA 2020, the systematic-review instrument.12 And the analysis section states the authors “meta-analyzed the findings to answer the research questions” — in a narrative and thematic synthesis where nothing was meta-analysed.1

Part C — For This Project

Section · 08

So What — For a Taiwan Music NPO

What it licenses, strongly. Across 26 English-language studies spanning 1997–2024, no study is set in Taiwan, China, Hong Kong, Japan, Korea or any other Asian country; the words Taiwan, Asia, Japan, Korea, Hong Kong and China do not appear in the article.13 The settings the supplement records are confined to the UK, US, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Switzerland, Germany, Spain, South Africa and Lithuania — none of them in Asia. (That list does not resolve to the paper’s stated nine countries, for the reason given in Section 06.) Combined with the null result from the eight North American knowledge stakeholders, this is a strong premise for the gap argument — stated correctly as: no mental health literacy research on artists has been published in the English-language indexed literature in any Asian setting, with the review’s own English-language restriction as the caveat. It is not a claim that none exists in Chinese.

What it does not license. Anything about what works. The review conducted no quality appraisal of the included studies — a stated limitation of scoping-review method — and so cannot speak to the strength of any evidence in it. Its evidence that literacy interventions help comes from athletes, medical students and schoolchildren, not artists.1 Cite it for the absence, never for an effect.

One concrete trap, worth the price of the reading. The MHL-Q19 (Guptill et al., 2022) is the obvious instrument to reach for when building a Taiwan baseline — and MHL there stands for Musicians’ Health Literacy, not mental health literacy. The review is explicit that it “asked questions about performance health issues and therefore, does not make any explicit inclusion of any of the components of mental health literacy,” while allowing that three of its items might be adaptable.18 Anyone selecting a measure on the name alone would field the wrong instrument. The review’s finding is that no validated instrument for mental health literacy in artists exists — so a Taiwan baseline would have to adapt a general-population instrument and validate it locally. That is a study in its own right, not a questionnaire to borrow.

The transplantable service hint. The one finding that recurs across the review’s music studies is that students take their health problems to their instrument teachers rather than to health professionals — reliance the review reads as “symptomatic of a general deficiency of information” (Williamon et al., 2006) — and that a music teacher with no psychotherapy training was nonetheless able to identify a student’s performance anxiety from its symptoms (Shaw et al., 2020). The review’s recommendation follows: raise teachers’ mental health literacy so they can recognise early signs.1 This is a music finding, not an all-disciplines one — no dance or visual-arts study in the included set supports it. In a Taiwan context that points at 術科老師 and 系上導師 rather than at a counselling centre — and it is cheap, specific, and does not require clinical capacity.

And the methodological lesson for our own work. The review demonstrates the failure mode of retrofitting a framework onto a literature that never used it: you produce counts that look like a map and are a record of your own coding generosity. If this project builds a Taiwan baseline, the construct has to be defined and measured prospectively. Coding backwards over existing 文獻 would reproduce exactly this paper’s problem.

Section · 09

Bottom Line

A review that is right about the gap and unreliable about its own measurements.

The gap claim survives everything: one seven-word definition in twenty-eight years of literature, no validated instrument, zero Asian settings, and eight stakeholders positioned to know who knew of nothing. That is a well-established absence, and it is the citation to take.

Everything quantitative around it should be handled with gloves: an abstract whose counts exceed the included set, percentages that contradict their own numerators, a flow diagram that does not balance, Methods that invert their own diagram, three incompatible lists of databases searched, self-contradicting eligibility criteria, and a four-component framework attributed to two papers that each enumerate six, neither of which lists the fourth.

Cite it for the hole in the literature and for the MHL-Q19 warning. Do not cite a number from it without re-deriving the number.
