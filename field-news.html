2026-08-26Abroad Research

India’s music industry gets its first nationwide mental-health dataset — and the numbers are the heaviest on this feed

Global Music Institute and TATVA in India, with Goldsmiths, University of London and Creative Empirical in the UK, published It’s Time to Talk: Mental Health in the Indian Music Industry, the first study of its kind in the country. Of 706 respondents who passed the exclusion criteria — 739 completed the survey, 1,321 opened it — 74.3% screened for abnormal psychological distress, 58.6% as at risk of suicide, 38.7% for anxiety and 36.8% for depression. Respondents aged 25 to 29 were the highest-risk band on all four measures; 89% of LGBTQIA+ respondents screened at risk of suicide against 55% of cis-het respondents; and 77% reported everyday discrimination, rising to 77.8% among those under 40 against 52.9% among those 40 and over. The report names the condition underneath the numbers a “crisis of occupational possibility” — the difficulty of turning musical commitment into work that can be sustained. Our reading: the transferable part is the build rather than the severity. Four partners, two grants, validated scales adapted to Indian measures of class, a survey deliberately written for managers, lawyers and educators as well as performers, and a practitioner toolkit shipped alongside the findings rather than promised after them. That is roughly the shape of the first serious piece of work a Taiwan organisation would have to commission. → Source

2026-07-02Abroad Research

A Rio orchestra study ties musicians’ pain to their finances, not only their posture

Kochem, Lima da Silva, Santos and Matei surveyed 273 professional orchestral musicians in Rio de Janeiro and found musculoskeletal pain close to universal: 93.4% reported it at some point and 86.8% within the previous twelve months. Six in ten, 60.4%, met the threshold for clinically significant music performance anxiety. Higher anxiety scores tracked greater pain intensity and greater interference with playing; women reported more pain, and weekly playing hours predicted interference. What separates this from the European literature sits in the life-events data — financial problems and separation or divorce each raised the odds of clinically significant performance anxiety. Our reading: this is the first middle-income-country dataset on this feed, and its framing is the one that travels. It measures physical injury, performance anxiety and money trouble with one instrument set instead of routing them to three different clinics, which is the only affordable way for a small organisation to look at an occupational-health problem at all. → Source

2026-08-20Abroad Funding

An industry-funded pot now buys specialist tour-health support for tours that cannot pay for it

The Music Industry Therapist Collective, a UK-based network of psychotherapists who work internationally with artists and crew, opened Pay It Forward with £272,000 from ten founding funders — AEG, d&b audiotechnik, Fly By Nite, Live Free 999, Live Nation, Lockton, Philip Selway of Radiohead, The Team and Universal Pixels. Up to 50 tours a year, and 150 over three years, receive a fully funded package: a Healthy Touring workshop for the team, an occupational-health consultation for the tour and production managers, ten confidential Tour Check-In sessions available around the clock before, during and after the run, written resources, and the Touring and Mental Health audiobook. The programme grew out of artists already paying for MITC’s touring support donating their unused check-ins to tours with fewer resources. Our reading: this is redistribution rather than hardship relief, and the part worth copying is that it buys the tour’s planning as well as the individual’s sessions — the occupational-health consultation goes to the people who set the schedule, which is where a touring team’s pressure is actually decided. → Source

2026-08-21Abroad Research

Charité’s musicians’-medicine centre puts prevalence numbers on 1,392 German musicians

Fernholz and colleagues at the Berlin Center for Musicians Medicine, Charité – Universitätsmedizin Berlin, surveyed 1,392 professional musicians and music students across Germany between January and June 2024, following up 327 of them in June 2025. Thirty per cent reported moderate-to-severe anxiety symptoms, 32% moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms, and 22% were carrying a currently diagnosed mental disorder; resilience, self-efficacy and self-esteem were each negatively associated with symptom load. The paper also reports treatment utilisation — who is and is not actually in care. This is the population-level counterpart to the same centre’s 678-patient clinic study already on this feed: that one describes who walks in, this one describes the field they walk in from. Our reading: for a Taiwan NPO the planning number is the treatment gap rather than the prevalence rate, because the gap is the part a service can close. → Source

2026-08-23Abroad Funding

Austin's musician health-insurance nonprofit is turning away hundreds as premiums jump

The Health Alliance for Austin Musicians — a roughly US$4 million-a-year programme that helps working musicians pay their marketplace insurance premiums, run alongside Central Health, the public agency covering low-income residents of Austin and Travis County — turned away hundreds of qualified applicants this year. Average 2026 marketplace premiums in the area rose about 58%, and HAAM members' own premiums about 60% year on year. Our reading: the instructive part here is the failure mode rather than the model. A subsidy programme sized to last year's prices runs out of room the moment the insurance market underneath it moves, and the waiting list, not the budget line, is where that shows up first — worth knowing before a Taiwan NPO designs anything that pays a third party's bill on a member's behalf. → Source

2026-01-22Abroad Research

A Scottish survey puts numbers on sexual violence in a national folk-music scene

Researchers at the Scottish Centre for Crime and Justice Research, University of Glasgow, surveyed 409 women musicians in Scottish folk and traditional music between May and December 2025 — 89% of them working musicians. Four in five, 81%, disclosed sexual violence or harassment in the scene; 72% of those experiences involved fellow musicians and peers and 53% involved prominent musicians in positions of power, 78% of respondents reported more than one incident, and 92% said more needs to be done. The report recommends codes of conduct, stronger reporting mechanisms and a designated safeguarding person on site at festivals and in teaching settings. Our reading: the study ran on a university seed-corn grant, which makes it the cheapest thing on this feed to copy — a scene-scoped survey is how an unmeasured harm becomes arguable, and the method travels to Taiwan more readily than any programme here. → Source

2026-07-29Abroad Policy

Colombia enacts a music law that builds the sector its own standing fund

Ley 2615, signed 2026-07-29 after passing the Chamber of Representatives on 4 June, creates a Fondo Cuenta Especial del Sector de la Música financed from streaming, advertising and ticket revenue and modelled on the country's film-development fund. It also exempts instruments and production hardware and software from VAT, tightens rules against illegitimate copyright collectors, sets a 40% quota for emerging and women artists at publicly funded festivals, and adds instrument-as-luggage and touring-visa provisions. To be exact about what it is not: no pension or health-coverage clauses appear in the law. Our reading: the clearest precedent yet for a state building a permanent, industry-financed money source for music workers rather than annual grants. → Source

2026-07-13Abroad Policy

The UK gives music freelancers a whistleblowing route they did not previously have

DCMS published “Turn It Up: Our Plan for Music”. Most of it is growth and export strategy, but two parts change conditions for workers: a Freelance Champion appointed to advocate for creative freelancers inside government, and the addition of CIISA, the creative-industries standards watchdog, to the UK's statutory list of prescribed persons and bodies — so freelancers, who lack ordinary employee protections, can now blow the whistle on harassment and bullying without fear of detriment or dismissal. Around 65% of the music and performing-arts workforce is freelance or self-employed. The accompanying £45M Music Growth Package is sector funding, not a worker-welfare fund. Our reading: the transferable move is procedural — giving freelancers a protected complaint route costs little and needs no new money. → Source

Lübeck publishes a psychological-stress inventory built for musicians rather than borrowed from the general population

Researchers at the University of Music Lübeck and the University of Lübeck published LIMIT, the Lübeck Inventory on Musicians' Psychological Stress — 34 items validated on 602 musicians, loading on four factors: self-efficacy, depression, time-management and pressure. It is built so teachers and institutions can screen musicians directly instead of adapting general-population scales. A measurement tool rather than a programme, which is why it travels: our reading, a Taiwan NPO or conservatory could translate and validate it in Mandarin and finally hold baseline numbers — the thing the Taiwan gap most lacks. → Source

A Berlin outpatient clinic for musicians reports diagnoses across 678 professional musicians

Fernholz and colleagues published results from an outpatient service specialising in musicians' health: of 678 professional musicians seen, 110 — 16.2 per cent — received a tentative mental-disorder diagnosis, most commonly anxiety, depressive and somatoform disorders. The paper is explicit that these are tentative diagnoses made by a non-psychiatrist physician, so the figure describes a clinic population, not population prevalence. Our reading: the dedicated musicians'-health clinic is itself the copyable structure, and the diagnosis mix tells a Taiwan NPO what to staff for. → Source

2025-07-28AbroadOccupational health

Musician's dystonia: a neurological occupational injury that has quietly ended careers — and that most of the industry never names

Yamaguchi is one of many. Focal (musician's) dystonia — a task-specific loss of fine-motor control built up over years of intensive practice, neurological, not psychological — has altered or ended the careers of pianists Leon Fleisher and Gary Graffman (who died in December 2025), guitarists Liona Boyd, David Leisner and Billy McLaughlin, oboist Alex Klein, and — disclosed candidly in 2025 — bassist Victor Wooten. It affects roughly 1% of professional musicians. The pattern is an occupational-health signal the field under-recognises: Germany treats it as a compensable occupational disease (Berufskrankheit 2115, since 2017); Taiwan, Japan, the UK, the US and most others do not — Taiwan's occupational-disease list covers ten ergonomic injuries, none of them neurological. The Taiwan so-what: occupational health is under-served in the music space here too, and simply naming dystonia correctly, plus early referral and prevention, is the low-cost place to start. → Full reading → Sources

2024-12-21AbroadResearch

A drummer turns his own dystonia into research — and returns to the stage by voice

Former RADWIMPS drummer Satoshi Yamaguchi developed lower-limb (right-foot) focal dystonia — a neurological occupational injury, not stage nerves — that forced an indefinite hiatus in 2015. Instead of disappearing, he joined Keio University's NeuroMusicLab and co-authored two 2024 case studies in Frontiers in Neurology that quantify how the condition corrupts a drummer's foot timing, then returned to performing in December 2024 through Yamaha's VXD, a voice-triggered bass drum. The signal isn't a cure — it's the bridge: musician → patient → researcher → performer. For Taiwan, where music occupational health is barely served, it models how lived experience can drive both research and destigmatisation. → Full reading → Source
