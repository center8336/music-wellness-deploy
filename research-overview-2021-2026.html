The database choice is itself a finding

The most-cited item in this set — Hesmondhalgh’s Is music streaming bad for musicians? — is not in PubMed, even though its journal New Media & Society has some 40 PubMed records. The venues carrying most of this work are barely indexed there: Poetics and Media, Culture & Society return zero PubMed records, Cultural Trends one. Others are indexed, but their musician-relevant articles are not what a biomedical query surfaces: The Sociological Review has 103 records, Cultural Sociology 6, the Journal of Cultural Economics 17. Either way, a PubMed-only search misses literature B almost entirely.6

§2 · Structure of the field

Three literatures that barely cite each other

The strongest structural finding of this sweep is not about musicians but about the research: work after 2021 splits into three bodies with different questions, methods and journals, and very little cross-citation.

A · Performance science

Largest by volume, and growing. Performance anxiety, flow, perfectionism, mindfulness, prevalence. Psychology of Music, Musicae Scientiae, Frontiers in Psychology. Cross-sectional, self-report, student-weighted.

B · Creative labour

Fastest-growing, and invisible to biomedical databases. Precarity, streaming economics, copyright assetization, gender and class, careers. New Media & Society, Cultural Trends, Poetics, The Sociological Review. Qualitative and political-economic.

C · Occupational health

The thinnest layer, and the one that would connect A to B. JOEM, Medical Problems of Performing Artists, J Occup Environ Hyg.

The split is by venue and method, and it is no longer backed by a count. The six-way topic classification earlier versions tallied here was retired on 2026-09-12 as unsound — it mixed publication form with topic and forced one label per record — and the corpus table now tags each record by study type and population, with no topic column.

Literature A measures how anxious musicians are. Literature B explains why the work is structured so as to produce anxiety. Kegelaers & Gibney’s 89 included studies come almost entirely from A and C — yet their own conclusion points at “structural changes within the industry,” which is literature B.1 §2’s split is ⚠ Inferred — our own reading of the corpus.

Musgrave’s two 2023 papers sit squarely in B and state the paradox this project is built on — music-making raises listeners’ wellbeing while damaging its makers’9 — and relocate the explanation from individual pathology to creative labour and relational work.10 Hesmondhalgh & Sun trace how that account finally reached mainstream policy attention.7

§3 · Established

What the evidence does establish

3.1  Elevated distress vs. the general workforce — on a narrow base

Vaag and colleagues compared 1,607 members of the Norwegian Musicians’ Union against 2,550 Norwegian workforce respondents (Level of Living 2012), adjusting for age, sex, education, smoking, alcohol, drug use, exercise and financial status. Psychological distress was more prevalent among the musicians, and highest among solo/lead performers, vocalists, keyboard players and musicians in the traditional-music genre.18

This is largely one cohort. The same n = 1,607 union sample underpins Vaag 2016 on distress, Vaag 2016 on psychotherapy and medication use25, Aalberg 2019 on demands and resources2, and Détári 2020 on the psychosocial work environment. The claim is replicated across analyses more than across populations; Denmark (n = 986) and Finland are the main independent national comparisons. ⚠ Inferred — our own observation from the four papers’ sampling descriptions.

3.2  The psychosocial profile is specific, not generic

Détári and colleagues compared 1,607 musicians against 8,517 general-workforce respondents and — unusually for this literature — reported effect sizes rather than only significance.3

The shape is high-demand / high-autonomy / low-support — not the low-control profile most occupational-stress instruments were built to detect. Within the sample, contemporary musicians were the most exposed subgroup, orchestral players had the lowest control, and soloists the lowest social support.3

3.3  A hazard that general work-environment surveys miss

Vastamäki and colleagues put all Finnish domestic symphony orchestras and a Finnish workforce sample through the same questionnaire. Noise disturbed the musicians 8× as often as controls and poor lighting 22× — expected. The result the authors flag as under-recognised is different: musicians reported mental violence or bullying at an odds ratio of 3.0 (95% CI 2.0–4.7). A workplace-psychosocial problem, in the most institutionally secure, salaried corner of the profession.

Two caveats the abstract does not carry. The data are old — the musician surveys were fielded in 2002 (n = 345) and 2011 (n = 195), against the Health 2000 and Health 2011 population surveys. And all three headline figures come from the 2002 contrast; the 2011 wave gives noise 16.3 (11.7–22.7), lighting 31.8 (21.2–47.6) and bullying OR 2.1 (1.2–3.7). The bullying excess holds in both waves but is smaller in the later one. This is the best available orchestral work-environment comparison, not a current measurement.19

3.4  Occupational stress has measurable structure

King, Koenig & Berg’s exploratory factor analysis of the Musician Occupational Stress Scale resolves “stress” into four factors accounting for roughly 50% of variance in MOSS items: work insecurity, touring, performance, and professional relationships. Unadjusted, every factor plus financial distress was associated with depression and anxiety, and touring stress additionally with alcohol misuse. But adjustment changes the story, and that is the point of the paper: for depression and anxiety, only work insecurity survives it (depression OR 5.42, 95% CI 3.23–9.09; anxiety OR 5.95, 3.51–10.11), while touring stress inverts (depression OR 0.59, 0.40–0.89). For alcohol misuse the surviving factor is different again — professional relationships (OR 1.66, 1.04–2.65) — and performance stress inverts (0.60, 0.40–0.91). The durable finding is job insecurity — an organisational variable, not a personal trait. The sample needs stating too: the 317 are the 48.4% who completed 655 invitations sent to musicians who had used mental-health services in the preceding two years, through a single US non-profit, fielded 2015 (the organisation is unnamed in the published abstracts). Exactly the selection effect §4.1 turns on.268

3.5  The first clinical sample

Almost everything above is self-report. Fernholz and colleagues report the exception: routine diagnostic data from 678 professional musicians and music students attending the Berlin Center for Musicians’ Medicine at Charité. Of these, 110 (16.2%) received a tentative psychiatric diagnosis, and 46 completed full psychiatric assessment (ICD-10 and SCID-II).4

Adjustment disorders accounted for 13% and somatoform disorders 11%. This is a clinic caseload, not a prevalence estimate — the denominator is musicians who sought help at a specialist service. Its value is the diagnostic composition, not the rates.

3.6  Income is not the whole story

The Danish When Music Speaks project surveyed 986 musicians across genres and career stages using HADS and Cantril’s ladder. Anxiety was predicted by age (40 or under), gender (female), and subjective career status — seeing music as one’s main career — but not by income. Depression was predicted by age and gender only. Subjective wellbeing was predicted by income (the three lowest categories), alongside age and gender. The authors’ own reading: elevated anxiety “cannot be entirely explained by musicians’ low and/or precarious incomes.”13

§4 · Not established

What the evidence does not establish

4.1  No probability-sample prevalence study exists — anywhere

Not in the UK, the US, Australia or Canada. Every headline percentage in circulation comes from a self-selected sample recruited through unions, charities or social media. The Norwegian and Danish studies are the closest to defensible national estimates, and both are union- or partnership-recruited rather than population-sampled. This project’s UK, US and Canada policy studies already record the same gap.

Practical consequence: quote the comparison — musicians against their own national workforce, same instrument, same year — not the rate.

4.2  The intervention evidence is thinner than its abstract

Nicholl & Abbott’s review is the first meta-analytic pooling of treatment for performance anxiety — not the first review; Fernholz et al. (2019), Brugués (2011) and Kenny (2005) preceded it without meta-analysis. The search found 57 intervention articles and 25 published RCTs; data for only 16 of them (total N = 543) could be obtained after contacting authors, and those 16 carry the pooled estimates.16

The between-groups difference is significant (p = .004), so treatment does beat control. But heterogeneity is high — Q(1) = 7.790, p = .005 — which the authors read as making it “difficult to isolate the ‘active ingredient(s)’”; the treatment prediction interval runs from −0.21 to 2.82, crossing zero; all but one study relied on participant self-report; delivery format and participant age were not significant moderators; and no head-to-head comparison of intervention types was possible.

So: something helps performance anxiety, roughly half the observed improvement is not attributable to the treatment, and nobody can yet say which component does the work. A programme funded on “CBT is proven for MPA” is running ahead of its evidence.

4.3  The mechanism is contested

Two 2025 papers point in different directions. Vachet’s study of semi-professional musicians aged 25–37 across seven locations — Paris, Brooklyn, San Francisco and Oakland, Portland, Stockholm, Reykjavík — argues that anxiety here is structurally produced: an outcome of precariousness that erodes ontological security, distributed unevenly by gender and class. Vachet writes against “an individualistic and biological framework” and for “a repoliticisation of emotions.”17 Musgrave, Gross & Carney’s Danish data finds income does not predict anxiety at all (§3.6).

Both cannot be straightforwardly true. Candidate reconciliations — that precarity is about unpredictability rather than income level, that Denmark’s welfare floor decouples the two, that “subjective career status” is the precarity variable that actually matters — are hypotheses, not findings. ⚠ Inferred — the characterisation of the dispute and all three reconciliations are ours. This is the most productive open question in the field.

4.4  Behind-the-stage workers are almost unstudied

For music-industry workers who are not performers — touring crew, management, label and venue staff — this sweep found essentially one evidence base: a single US touring sample (Newman et al.: 63 artists and 443 crew members, re-analysed for trait mindfulness by Bergson et al.14) plus one multiwave study of music-industry workers during COVID.22 The touring sample itself is Newman et al.15 Crew outnumber artists roughly seven to one in that very sample, which makes this the clearest unclaimed gap in the field.

4.5  The evidence comes from a narrow set of countries

Kegelaers & Gibney’s 89 included studies break down as United Kingdom 14, Germany 13, United States 12, Australia 8, Norway 6, then Canada, Finland, the Netherlands and Spain at 4 each, with China 1 and Singapore 1.1 The 2026 arrivals begin to shift East Asian representation — a Shanghai study of female creative workers,30 a Japanese performance-anxiety study and a Simplified-Chinese validation of a performance-pain instrument in Shandong.29

This is a description of the studies found, not of what exists. It rests on titles and abstracts plus the spine review’s own country table — not on an affiliation search: the Web of Science export carried no affiliation fields.

§5 · 2025–2026

What changed in the last twelve months

Four shifts, all recent enough that a 2024 literature review would have missed them.

  1. Clinical data arrived. Fernholz et al. (§3.5) is the first study in this space built on clinician-assigned diagnoses rather than screening questionnaires.
  2. Occupational health reviewed the field on its own terms. Fukumura et al.’s mapping review in the Journal of Occupational and Environmental Hygiene approaches the same corpus from industrial hygiene rather than music psychology.5
  3. Literatures A and B began converging. Vachet (2025) explains anxiety through precarity, gender and class in a mainstream sociology journal; Musgrave’s Creative Labour Sustainability Framework (2026) turns the structural account into a governance-oriented risk assessment for harm prevention;12 and a 2026 Routledge volume on recovery in the performing arts carries a chapter by Altenmüller on orchestral musicians, alongside one on recovery in professional musicians — the first time this project has found orchestral employers addressed as a unit of intervention in a book aimed at clinicians. That framing is our reading of the volume, not a phrase in the chapter titles.28
  4. Fifteen reviews in two years, ten of them in 2026 alone. The corpus holds 17 reviews across 2021–2026, distributed 0, 1, 1, 0, 5, 10 by year. The 2026 cohort covers performance-anxiety treatment, alcohol and drug use, metal musicians’ health, artists and eating disorders, psychosocial injury-risk screening, social support in performance anxiety, mindfulness- and acceptance-based interventions, flow and performing hurt — plus the mapping review27 — and, separately, a review of musicians and suicide.11 The field was not unreviewed before this — Willis et al. (2019) systematically reviewed occupational demands and performing-artist wellbeing24, and performance-anxiety reviews run back to Kenny (2005), as Nicholl & Abbott themselves record16 — but the 2025–2026 cluster is the first to cover the field domain by domain and the first to attempt meta-analysis: the signature of a literature large enough to synthesise, and still short of the primary studies worth synthesising.
§6 · Method in the literature

The comparison design

The strongest studies in this literature are comparisons, not censuses — the same validated work-environment instrument given to musicians and to a national workforce sample in the same period.

Vaag measured distress with the Hopkins Symptom Checklist-25 against Norway’s Level of Living survey; Détári used items from the JCQ and QPS Nordic against the same national frame; Vastamäki used a shared questionnaire against a Finnish workforce sample. The design carries a warning: Détári’s musician data are from 2013 and the workforce comparison from 2009–10, a three-to-four-year gap the paper does not acknowledge, so period effects could confound it. Vaag’s waves do line up (musicians February–April 2013; Level of Living fielded October 2012–January 2013). Vastamäki is the cleanest instance of the design — the same work-environment questions, waves aligned — and its limitation is vintage, not method (§3.3). Détári has the shared work-environment items but a period gap; Vaag has aligned waves but a symptom checklist. The design is proven; its cleanest execution is two decades old.
