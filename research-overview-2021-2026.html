§2 · Structure of the field

Three literatures that barely cite each other

A · Performance science / music psychology

The largest. Performance anxiety, flow, perfectionism, prevalence. Cross-sectional, self-report.

B · Creative labour / cultural sociology

Precarity, streaming, gender and class. Qualitative.

C · Occupational health

Playing-related pain, hearing, dystonia. The first search's keyword rule discarded it; the occupational-disease arm's first screening phase added 77 rows, with about 166 candidates still unscreened.

A measures how anxious musicians are; B explains why the work produces it. Kegelaers & Gibney's 89 studies come almost entirely from A and C, yet conclude that "structural changes within the industry" are needed — which is B's subject.

§3 · Established

What the evidence establishes

3.1  More distress than the general workforce — on one cohort

1,607 Norwegian union musicians vs 2,550 workforce respondents, adjusted for age, sex, education, substance use, exercise and finances: distress higher, highest in solo/lead performers, vocalists, keyboard players and traditional-genre musicians (Vaag et al., 2016a)21. The same cohort underpins Vaag et al. (2016b)22, Aalberg et al. (2019)1 and Détári et al. (2020)4; Denmark (n = 986) and Finland are the main independent comparisons.

3.2  High demand, high control, low support

Same cohort vs 8,517 workforce respondents (Détári et al., 2020)4:

Not the low-control profile most occupational-stress instruments were built to detect.

3.3  Bullying in the most secure jobs

Finnish symphony orchestras vs the Finnish workforce, same questionnaire (Vastamäki et al., 2023)24: noise 8×, poor lighting 22×, mental violence or bullying OR 3.0 (95% CI 2.0–4.7). These are the 2002 wave (n = 345, against Health 2000); in 2011 (n = 195, against Health 2011) bullying was OR 2.1 (1.2–3.7), noise 16.3 (11.7–22.7), lighting 31.8 (21.2–47.6).

3.4  For depression and anxiety, only job insecurity survives adjustment

Four stress factors — work insecurity, touring, performance, professional relationships (King et al., 2024)11. Adjusted, work insecurity alone predicts depression (OR 5.42, 3.23–9.09) and anxiety (OR 5.95, 3.51–10.11); touring stress inverts (depression OR 0.59, 0.40–0.89). For alcohol misuse, professional relationships survive (OR 1.66, 1.04–2.65) and performance stress inverts (0.60, 0.40–0.91). Sample: 317 of 655 US musicians invited because they had used mental-health services in the preceding two years, fielded 2015 (Berg et al., 2022)2 — the selection §4.1 turns on.

3.5  A clinical caseload

678 musicians and students at Charité's musicians' clinic; 46 fully assessed (Fernholz et al., 2026)6: depression 39%, performance anxiety 26%, adjustment disorder 13%, somatoform 11%; 28% personality disorder or accentuation; 41.3% two or more diagnoses. Diagnostic composition, not prevalence.

3.6  Income predicts wellbeing, not anxiety

986 Danish musicians: anxiety predicted by age ≤ 40, female gender and subjective career status; depression by age and gender; wellbeing by low income (Musgrave et al., 2025)14.

§4 · Not established

What it does not establish

4.1  Mental-health prevalence

No probability-sample study exists in any country; every headline mental-health rate comes from a self-selected sample. Quote the comparison, not the rate.

4.2  Which intervention works

Nicholl & Abbott (2026)17 found 25 performance-anxiety RCTs and obtained data for 16 (N = 543). Treatment g = 1.21 [0.89, 1.53]; control g = 0.57 [0.28, 0.86]; difference p = .004; treatment prediction interval −0.21 to 2.82. Something helps; about half the improvement is not the treatment; the authors could not isolate the active component. A second 2026 meta-analysis covers mindfulness- and acceptance-based interventions only (Zhang et al., 2026)28.

4.3  The mechanism

Vachet (202523; semi-professional musicians in seven locations) argues anxiety is produced by precarity, unevenly by gender and class; the Danish survey finds income does not predict it (§3.6). Two reconciliations — precarity as unpredictability rather than low income; a welfare floor decoupling the two — are hypotheses. This is the field's most productive open question.

4.4  Workers beyond the performer are barely studied

Mental health: one international touring survey of 1,154 artists and crew (Newman et al., 202215, 508 completers; re-analysed by Bergson et al., 20243, n = 550), one COVID multiwave study (Scott et al., 2024)18, and interviews and focus groups with 15 professionals including producers and managers (Jepson et al., 2025)9. Hearing: 27 live-music sound engineers, 30% with constant tinnitus (McGinnity et al., 2021)12. Work conditions: producers (Ng & Gamble, 202616; Woods & Davis, 2026)26, composers (Dowd & Park, 2024)5, songwriters (Smolka & Heugens, 2026)19. The job-title arm (§1) named crew, roadies and tour managers; none of its 61 records new to the corpus studied them.

4.5  Countries

Kegelaers & Gibney's 89 studies: UK 14, Germany 13, US 12, Australia 8, Norway 6, Canada / Finland / Netherlands / Spain 4 each, China 1, Singapore 1. The corpus cannot update this count: its Web of Science exports carry no affiliations. East Asian samples appear in its 2025–2026 titles and abstracts (e.g. Yang, 202627, Shanghai; Takagi et al., 202620, Japan). English-language record only.

§5 · Recent change

What changed in 2025–2026

  1. Clinical data (§3.5).
  2. Occupational health reviewed the field — a mapping review in J Occup Environ Hyg (Fukumura et al., 2026)7.
  3. A and B converging — Vachet (2025)23 in mainstream sociology; Musgrave (2026)13 recasting the structural account as a risk assessment.
  4. Reviews multiplied — 24 corpus rows typed Review across 2021–2026 (0, 3, 2, 0, 8, 11 by year); three 2026 reviews are meta-analyses. Earlier reviews exist (Willis et al., 201925; performance-anxiety reviews back to 2005, per Nicholl & Abbott). A literature large enough to synthesise, still short of primary studies worth synthesising.
§6 · Design

The comparison design

The strongest studies give one instrument to musicians and to a national workforce sample. Vastamäki's waves align but are two decades old (§3.3); Vaag's align (musicians Feb–Apr 2013; workforce Oct 2012–Jan 2013) but use a symptom checklist; Détári compares 2013 musicians with a 2009–10 workforce, a gap the paper does not acknowledge.
