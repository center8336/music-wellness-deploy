Reviewed by 陳棉 · 2026-09-12

↓ Scroll
Part A — Framing

Section · 01

Why Read This

Read the Frontiers Paper →

This is the paper the field will cite when it wants a number for musician suicide, and it is the only one that proposes a named, off-the-shelf prevention architecture — the Zero Suicide Framework — for the music industries. For a Taiwan NPO deciding what to build, those are the two questions it appears to answer: how bad is it, and what do we do.

The reading below finds that it answers the second question more honestly than the first, and that both answers are smaller than the paper's reception is likely to make them.

There is a second reason to read it closely. This is the nearest thing in the literature to an Asian-context intervention frame, through its Korean material. That material turns out not to be what it looks like.

Section · 02

What This Paper Is

It is a narrative position piece, and the authors say so twice. In the conclusion: “this paper is a cross-disciplinary position piece and is neither a systematic nor scoping review.”1

And at the head of the prevention half, more consequentially: the approaches that follow “are not based on empirical evidence specific to musicians or other entertainers, nor have they been evaluated in peer reviewed scholarship among this population,” and constitute — in NICE's language — “‘expert opinion’ as opposed to Level 1/2/3 evidence.”1

Frontiers nevertheless labels it TYPE Review; received 2024-10-08, accepted 2025-01-10, published 2025-03-07, with publication costs paid by Goldsmiths, University of London.1

That label, plus a structured abstract and a dense epidemiological opening, will do most of the work in how the paper is read. Both disclaimers sit where a skim will miss them — one in the preamble to Section 3, one in the penultimate sentence of the conclusion. The genre matters: a reader who takes the opening figures as a review's yield rather than as an author's selection will over-weight them.

Part B — The Evidence, Traced

Section · 03

The Headline Number, Checked

The paper's most quotable sentence places three statistics in one paragraph: the US total age-adjusted suicide rate in 2022 was 14.2 per 100,000; and Sussell et al. “found that musicians, singers, and related workers (138.7 per 100,000) had the third highest suicide rate among major occupation groups only behind logging workers (161.1) and agricultural and food scientists (173.1).”1

Checked against those sources themselves, three things go wrong.

The comparison is between different populations. The 14.2 figure is the 2022 all-ages, both-sex, age-adjusted US rate from NCHS — where the male rate was 23.0 and the female rate 5.9.4 The 138.7 figure is a male-only, crude rate from 2021 death data, in a study whose own denominator is the civilian working population aged 16–64 — a population whose male rate is 32.0 (95% CI 31.6–32.4).2 Laid side by side as the paragraph lays them, the invited arithmetic is ≈9.8×. Against the correct internal baseline it is ≈4.3×. Still a serious elevation; less than half the one the paragraph implies.

“Major occupation groups” is wrong. The MMWR table is headed “by sex, for 22 major occupation groups and detailed groups with rates higher than all occupations combined,” and its text is explicit that these are “detailed occupational groups.”2 Musicians, singers and related workers is a detailed occupation nested inside the major group “Arts, design, entertainment, sports, and media.” All three of the paper's comparators are detailed groups. The error inflates the apparent standing of the finding.

The ranking is an artefact of point estimates. The published intervals are musicians 138.7 (113.0–164.4), logging workers 161.1 (106.8–234.7), agricultural and food scientists 173.1 (94.1–294.6).2 Every one of those intervals overlaps every other one, end to end — and the MMWR itself footnotes that caution is warranted for detailed groups with wide intervals, “which can be due to relatively low numbers of decedents and working populations.”2 “Third highest” is a ranking of midpoints that the data do not support. The defensible claim is that musicians sit in a band of occupations with markedly elevated male suicide rates — not that they are third.

Section · 04

The England Figure Belongs to a Larger Group

The paper reports that for England 2011–2015, within “culture, media and sport occupations” the male suicide rate was 20% above the male average and the female rate 69% above, and that musicians, actors and entertainers were the highest-risk group within it.1

That sentence is technically accurate — “in which” refers to the parent category — but it reads as though 20%/69% describes musicians. It does not. The ONS attaches those figures to the whole culture, media and sport group; the paper's own footnote gives that group's SMR = 120 (95% CI 107–135).3 One further wrinkle the paper does not carry: ONS names musicians, actors and entertainers as the highest-risk subgroup behind the male figure, but attributes the female elevation to “artistic, literary and media occupations” — a differently drawn category.3

No musician-specific SMR appears in the paper's main text, and there is a reason. The ONS states that it comments only on occupations with 50 or more suicides, “as their SMRs can be estimated more precisely,” and warns that a recorded occupation may reflect a lifetime job rather than the job held at death.3

A musician-only figure exists in the underlying academic paper — Windsor-Shellard & Gunnell (2019), The British Journal of Psychiatry — which is paywalled. The two secondary values we could locate for it disagree with each other, and neither is reported here.3 Unresolved, and worth resolving before anyone quotes a musician-specific English SMR.

Section · 05

The Korean Material Has No Korean Prevalence Data

The reading queue reached for this paper as the nearest transplantable Asian-context frame. Read closely, it is not one — and the authors are straightforward about why.

On Korea: “at present there is no available data (that we are aware of) on whether the prevalence of suicide is higher among Korean musicians than the general public in Korea, which has one of the highest suicide rates in the world.”1

What Korea supplies is a set of hypothesised, culturally specific risk factors — obsessive fandom and cyberbullying read through a Durkheimian lens, early-childhood training regimes, gruelling promotional schedules. And then the paper concedes the point that matters most to us: some researchers hold that “these challenges might not be unique to these musicians per se but are instead amplified reflections of trends for high-pressured, socially-prescribed perfectionism in spheres of work and education in Korea more generally.”1

That is the floor-versus-room question in a different jurisdiction. If the driver is the society rather than the industry, a music-specific service is treating an amplifier, not a source — and the intervention that moves the number is general, not musical. The paper raises this and moves on. For a Taiwan NPO it is the single most useful sentence in the article, and it cuts against the music-specific case rather than for it.

Section · 06

Two Further Strains, and a Disclosure

The “Identify” element rests on a predictor the paper says barely works. Table 1 gives Element 3's music-specific adaptation as “applying risk factors from Franklin et al. to musicians; understanding warning signs.”1 Two pages later the same meta-analysis is quoted concluding that “prediction was only slightly better than chance,” and the paper adds that our ability to determine who will attempt or die by suicide “is not reliable.”1 The authors flag the tension honestly and then keep the element unchanged. An organisation building a screening programme on Element 3 should know it is building on a foundation the authors have already described as barely better than chance.

The 17.4% attempt figure is conditioned on prior treatment. The paper reports that a survey by Berg et al. found “17.4% of respondents had a history of at least one suicide attempt, although larger and more representative datasets are limited.”1 “Limited” understates it. That survey was emailed in March–April 2015 to 655 musicians who had accessed mental-health services in the preceding two years through a single Austin, Texas non-profit; 317 completed it (48.4%).5 The sample is selected on prior service use, from one city, a decade before the citing paper. Its companion prevalence figures — over 36% moderate-to-severe depression, 33.5% anxiety, 65.4% positive on AUDIT-C — are what that selection produces.5 The 17.4% is a fact about help-seeking Austin musicians in 2015, not about musicians.

A disclosure worth naming, plainly. Both authors are listed on Backline's Clinical Advisory Board on Backline's own team page,6 and the paper names Backline first among five organisations “showing leadership in this area.”1 The declared conflict-of-interest statement reads: “The authors declare that the research was conducted in the absence of any commercial or financial relationships that could be construed as a potential conflict of interest.”1 Frontiers' form asks about commercial and financial relationships, so an unpaid advisory role plausibly falls outside what it requires. This is not an allegation of wrongdoing. It is a fact a reader weighing whether to adopt the framework should have, and it is not in the paper.

Part C — Appraisal

Section · 07

What the Paper Gets Right

Three things, and they are not small.

It refuses the romance. The opening dismantles the ‘27 Club’ and the “tortured artist” trope with evidence rather than distaste, noting that the empirical record shows no spike in death at twenty-seven.1 For a field whose public conversation runs on mythology, that is useful work.

The methodological section is genuinely critical — including of the authors' own field. It walks through coroner classification driving coding: Hutchence recorded as suicide, Winehouse as alcohol poisoning, Nick Drake's overdose as suicide, Howie Epstein's not — concluding that many suicides are mis-classified as accidental overdoses.1 It then names the biographical-study problem: eminence is unmeasurable when only the famous have biographies. And it states that the studies by Gross and Musgrave, Loveday, and Musgrave et al. “rely on self-selecting samples,” with only Vaag's Norwegian work making an explicit comparison to the general public.1 The first author is criticising his own prior work by name. That is rare and creditable.

The ZSF mapping is the right shape even where it is unevidenced. Seven elements, each with a concrete music-industry adaptation, is a far more actionable artefact than another call for awareness. It is a hypothesis set, properly labelled as one.

Section · 08

So What — For a Taiwan Music NPO

The framework's centre of gravity is a problem Taiwan does not have. Element 1 (Leadership) spends most of its length on affordability and insurance: musicians “may lack access to quality health insurance,” and leaders should connect them to professionals who “can do so affordably.”1 That is a US floor problem. Taiwan's National Health Insurance already supplies that floor. Importing the ZSF wholesale would put the most effort into the element Taiwan needs least — the standing failure mode this project has been calling floor versus room.

Two elements transplant cleanly and cheaply. Element 2 (Training) — gatekeeper training for the people already around musicians: managers, booking agents, tour managers, label and venue staff — is industry-shaped, requires no clinical licence, and the paper marshals real evidence that asking directly about suicide has no iatrogenic effect.1 Element 6 (Transition) — continuity of care across touring, warm hand-offs, bridge appointments, telehealth — is the other. Both are exactly the room an NPO can build without standing up clinical services.

Elements 3–5 are clinical and should stay clinical. C-SSRS, the Safety Planning Intervention, DBT, CT-SP, CAMS, CBT-SP: all belong with licensed providers. The paper notes the C-SSRS “has not been evaluated among musicians.”1 An NPO's role here is referral and route-in, not delivery.

Build Element 7 first, not last. The paper's own bottom line is that none of this has been evaluated in musicians. Any organisation adopting the ZSF is therefore running an unevaluated programme. That inverts the running order: measurement is not the seventh thing you do, it is the precondition for claiming the other six worked.

And note what suicide prevention is, structurally. It is a floor question — means, crisis response, mortality — and in Taiwan the floor is largely 衛福部's to hold. The room an NPO can occupy is narrower and more specific: music-literate competence, a route in, and someone who knows what a tour schedule does to a treatment plan. This paper is more useful as a map of that division than as a programme to copy.

Section · 09

Bottom Line

A creditable, honest position piece whose disclaimers are more load-bearing than its statistics — and whose statistics do not survive contact with their own sources intact. The 138.7 figure is real, but it is a male-only crude rate in a detailed occupation with an interval that overlaps its neighbours, and the ≈10× comparison the paper's paragraph invites is closer to ≈4.3×. The Korean section, reached for as an Asian evidence base, states plainly that no Korean prevalence data exists.

What survives is the ZSF mapping — a well-shaped, explicitly unevaluated hypothesis set — and one sentence about Korea that reframes the whole question for a Taiwan NPO: if the pressure is the society rather than the industry, a music-specific service is treating the amplifier.

Cite this paper for the framework and for the methodological critique. Do not cite it for a rate.
